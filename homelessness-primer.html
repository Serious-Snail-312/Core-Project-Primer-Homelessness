National Context

On a single night in January 2024, 771,480 people experienced homelessness in the U.S., the highest figure the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) has ever recorded. Analysts point to a combination of structural forces: an affordable housing shortage, inflation, stagnant wages among low- and middle-income households, and the enduring effects of systemic racism. Pandemic-era protections such as eviction moratoriums and expanded Child Tax Credits also expired, while disasters, public-health shocks, and immigration dynamics strained local systems.

Key Definitions

HUD's core categories include: (1) Literally Homeless (sheltered or unsheltered), (2) Imminent Risk of Homelessness, (3) Certain youth and families under other federal statutes with persistent instability, and (4) Fleeing or Attempting to Flee Domestic Violence. PIT (Point-in-Time) counts primarily enumerate Category 1, meaning many precariously housed households are not in the PIT numerator despite being at risk and eligible for some services through other programs.

Sheltered vs. Unsheltered

  • Unsheltered: Sleeping in places "not meant for human habitation" (e.g., streets, parks, vehicles).
  • Sheltered: Emergency shelter, safe haven, or transitional housing on the night of the PIT count.

San Francisco's Continuum of Care (CoC) is a group of organizations serving their community's homeless population, led by the city's Department of Homelessness and Supportive Housing (HSH) and overseen by the Local Homeless Coordinating Board (LHCB)—entities that coordinate PIT methodology and system strategy and publish routine data.

Causes of Homelessness

In their book Homelessness is a Housing Problem, Gregg Colburn and Clayton Page Aldern argue that median rents in a city are the strongest predictor of that city's homelessness problem, while other common explanations, such as poverty, drug use, or systemic injustice, fail to account for regional variation in homelessness rates. In fact, Colburn and Aldern provide evidence that poverty rates in cities are negatively correlated with homelessness. They claim rising housing prices and a lack of affordable housing supply are the primary drivers of homelessness.

New research using linked administrative data indicates that homelessness often arises in the context of long-term, severe material deprivation. Many (roughly half) people experiencing homelessness report some level of employment, and more than 90% report receiving either wages or social safety-net benefits. However, these sources of income are too persistently low to sustain stable housing. Thus, homelessness is a result of prolonged deprivation, not just sudden shocks.

Individual factors such as mental illness or drug use can exacerbate extended periods of poverty, but do not account for the full scope of the homelessness problem in America or in San Francisco. Nationally, the share of adults experiencing homelessness with a serious mental illness (22%) or a chronic substance use disorder (18%) is substantial but still a minority of the overall population counted in 2024 (with overlap between these groups). Behavioral health is consequently part of the story, but not the full narrative.

Homelessness is disproportionately concentrated among people of color and marginalized groups such as individuals with disabilities or youth with gender identities outside of the binary.

Scope of Homelessness in San Francisco

San Francisco's latest HUD-mandated Point-in-Time (PIT) count was conducted the night of January 30, 2024. It found that 8,323 people were experiencing homelessness, 52% (4,354 people) of whom were unsheltered. According to follow-up survey work, 66% of respondents said their current spell of homelessness had lasted a year or more. Over the course of an entire year, more than 20,000 people seek homeless services, far more than the one-night snapshot captures.

Unsheltered homelessness in the 2024 count dropped 16% from 2019, while sheltered homelessness increased 39% since 2019. According to the PIT count report, people experiencing homelessness in San Francisco are more likely to be sheltered than ever before. The city attributes this to expanded shelter inventory and utilization.

Historical and Economic Context

Homelessness has been a significant issue in the Bay Area since the late 1970s. San Francisco mayors have cycled through short-term emergency responses, service expansion, punitive enforcement, and housing-focused strategies, yet none have credibly reversed the city's overall trajectory of homelessness. Early administrations like Feinstein's (1978–88) treated homelessness as temporary and relied heavily on shelters and short-stay hotel programs. Successors Agnos (1988–92) and Jordan (1992–96) swung between social-service improvements and criminalization strategies, including the Matrix Program, while Brown (1996–2004) expanded affordable housing and ultimately abandoned the effort as "unsolvable." Mayors Newsom (2004–10), Lee (2011–17), and Breed (2017–25) sought to improve access to services through navigation centers, expanded shelter capacity, and coordinated entry to consolidate service providers. Despite these efforts, the city's daily homeless population has remained high, with inflow persistently outpacing exits.

According to Zillow data, typical prices for homes in the lower part of the pricing distribution (5th to 35th percentiles) in San Francisco more than tripled from $221,812 in April 2012 to a peak of $747,901 in June 2022. While this meteoric rise in home prices has halted in recent years as tech workers have faced accelerating layoffs, the AI boom has ensured that both rents and home prices remain high, and more San Francisco families are having difficulty affording housing. According to the National Low Income Housing Coalition, San Francisco only has 32 available affordable rental units for every 100 extremely low-income households.

Recent Changes Under Mayor Lurie

Mayor Daniel Lurie campaigned on issues of public safety and the need for more housing. He refers to homelessness in San Francisco as "a Fentanyl crisis" (6:30 mark). He has begun implementing a strategy he calls "Breaking the Cycle," which provides for more interim housing and treatment beds, coordinating street outreach across agencies and within neighborhoods, and piloting new stabilization or triage centers. The city claims to have opened at least 600 new, treatment-focused beds. City-funded programs are also now prohibited from distributing safe drug-use supplies without counseling or connections to treatment.

These changes have already had some impacts visible in the data: visible tents reported by the city have fallen to multi-year lows. Advocates suggest that this decrease in tents may be due to "the homeless encampment sweeps the city started during the London Breed administration in August 2024, where workers, newly emboldened by a U.S. Supreme Court decision, confiscated tents and belongings," rather than an improvement in access to shelter.

Selected Open Questions

  • Enforcement vs. engagement. After recent court decisions, cities have greater leeway to enforce encampment restrictions. Supporters argue such restrictions improve safety and provide a pathway to services; critics warn that enforcement without guaranteed and acceptable shelter is cruel, creates churn in the shelter system, and erodes trust.
  • Interim vs. permanent housing. Interim housing models such as motels or tiny homes can be delivered in months rather than years, potentially at lower cost. But without rapid, reliable routes to permanent housing ("exits"), interim housing can lengthen stays and tie up scarce operating dollars.
  • Measurement. PIT counts, tent tallies, and "encampment engagement" metrics each tell partial stories. Methodological updates (e.g., better enumeration of families and people in vehicles) may correct past undercounts but can also be read as "data gaming" in polarized discourse. Expect arguments over what success should be measured by—reduced inflow, shorter time homeless, stable exits, reduced mortality and harms, or cleaner streets—and in what balance.
  • Harm reduction. Advocates of harm reduction policies argue that programs such as needle exchanges keep the community safe from infectious diseases or tainted drugs. Critics aim to incentivize seeking out and receiving treatment, arguing that this may be the most compassionate solution, even if the treatment is not voluntary.
